## Definition

**Brahman** (*brahman*; ब्रह्मन्) is the name the Upaniṣads and the Vedānta schools give to ultimate reality. The Upaniṣads identify brahman with the self ([[atman|ātman]]), and suggest that knowing it saves one from all sorrow.[^2] In [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]] it is the principle of nonduality revealed by the Upaniṣads, the reality of all things.[^3]

## Etymology

The Monier-Williams dictionary derives *brahman* from the verbal root *bṛh*, and gives its literal sense as "growth", "expansion", "evolution", "development", "swelling of the spirit or soul".[^1] The word has a wide range of senses. Among the first listed are a pious utterance or prayer; the sacred word, the Veda or a sacred text; the Brāhmaṇa portion of the Veda; the sacred syllable *om*; and religious or spiritual knowledge.[^1]

## In the scriptures

The Upaniṣads identify brahman with the self.[^2] The [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]], a foundational text of Vedānta, open with an enquiry into brahman. Their second sūtra characterises it as that from which "the origin, subsistence, and dissolution" of the world proceed, in George Thibaut's translation.[^7]

## Interpretations across traditions

The Vedānta schools all take brahman, as taught in the Upaniṣads, as their subject.[^2] They differ on how brahman relates to individual selves and to the world:

- **Advaita Vedānta.** Brahman is the fundamental reality underlying all objects and experiences: pure existence, pure consciousness and pure bliss.[^4] Brahman appears as the manifold objects of experience through its creative power, [[maya|māyā]], and the individual self is in reality identical with brahman.[^4] For [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]], the essential core of the self is brahman, and the self is pure, non-intentional consciousness.[^3]
- **[[vishishtadvaita|Viśiṣṭādvaita]].** For [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]], brahman stands to all other beings as the soul or mind stands to its body. This is the doctrine of body and soul (*śarīra* and *śarīrī*), and the school's name means "qualified non-dualism", or "non-duality of the qualified whole".[^5]
- **[[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]].** For [[madhvacharya|Madhva]], there are two orders of reality. The independent (*svatantra*) consists of brahman alone; the dependent (*paratantra*) consists of selves (*jīva*) and lifeless matter (*jaḍa*). The scriptures teach a fivefold difference: between Viṣṇu and selves, Viṣṇu and matter, selves and matter, one self and another, and one form of matter and another.[^6]

## Related concepts

Brahman is closely tied to [[atman|ātman]], the self, which the Upaniṣads identify with it.[^2] In Advaita it is also tied to [[maya|māyā]], the power through which brahman appears as the world.[^4]
