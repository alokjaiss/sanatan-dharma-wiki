## Overview

The **Yoga Sutras** (*Yogasūtra*; योगसूत्र) of [[patanjali|Patañjali]] are a compact work of about 1,200 words in 195 sūtras.[^1] They are the foundational text of the [[yoga-darshana|Yoga]] darśana, one of the six āstika schools of Hindu philosophy.[^2] Patañjali defines yoga in the second sūtra:

> योगश्चित्तवृत्तिनिरोधः
>
> *yogaś cittavṛttinirodhaḥ*
>
> "Yoga is the restriction of the fluctuations of mind-stuff." (tr. J. H. Woods, 1914)
>
> — Yoga Sūtra 1.2[^3]

## Structure and contents

The sūtras fall into four chapters (*pāda*):[^1][^4]

- **Samādhi pāda** (51 sūtras) defines yoga as the complete cessation of the active states of mind, and outlines the stages of insight that follow from it.
- **Sādhana pāda** (55 sūtras) sets out the practices, and the moral and ethical observances, that must come before serious meditation.
- **Vibhūti pāda** (55 sūtras) deals mainly with the extraordinary powers that can come to a practitioner whose mind is deeply concentrated.
- **Kaivalya pāda** (34 sūtras) deals with liberation and contains, among other things, Patañjali's response to Buddhist challenges.

## Key teachings

The text rests on a dualist picture of reality, shared with [[samkhya|Sāṅkhya]]: *prakṛti*, the primordial material matrix of the universe, and *puruṣa*, the innumerable conscious selves embedded within it. The aim of yoga is to free the self from its entanglement with matter.[^1]

The practice is set out in eight limbs (*aṣṭāṅga*, 2.29): abstentions (*yama*), observances (*niyama*), posture (*āsana*), breath control (*prāṇāyāma*), withdrawal of the senses (*pratyāhāra*), concentration (*dhāraṇā*), meditation (*dhyāna*) and absorption (*samādhi*).[^1][^4] Patañjali also allows that the goal can be reached through devotion to the Lord, Īśvara (1.23).[^1][^4] Īśvara is a special self, untouched by karma and the afflictions, omniscient, and the teacher of the ancients.[^1]

## Authorship and dating

### Traditional account

Tradition, first evidenced in King Bhoja's 11th-century commentary, holds that Patañjali was the same person as the author of the great commentary on Pāṇini's grammar, and also ascribes a treatise on medicine to him.[^1] Patañjali is not the founder of yoga, whose origins long preceded him.[^1]

### Historical perspective

The date cannot be fixed precisely. Most scholars place the text shortly after the turn of the common era, around the 1st–2nd century CE, though it has been dated as early as several centuries BCE; it is no later than the 5th century CE.[^1]

## Commentaries and interpretations

The oldest surviving commentary, the [[yoga-bhashya|Yoga Bhāṣya]] ascribed to the legendary Vyāsa and usually dated to around the 4th–5th century CE, became almost as authoritative as the sūtras: later commentators elaborated on Vyāsa's reading.[^1] Other important commentaries are Vācaspati Miśra's *Tattvavaiśāradī* (9th century), King Bhoja's *Rājamārtaṇḍa* (11th century) and Vijñānabhikṣu's *Yogavārttika*.[^1] A commentary called the *Vivaraṇa* is attributed to Śaṅkara, but its authenticity has been questioned since 1927 and remains unresolved.[^1]

## Influence

Bryant notes that presenting yoga as mainly or even only bodily posture (*āsana*) is essentially a modern Western development, with no precedent in the premodern tradition.[^1]
