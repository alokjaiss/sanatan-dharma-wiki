## Overview

The **Brahma Sutras** (*Brahmasūtra*; ब्रह्मसूत्र), "The Aphorisms on Brahman", were composed by [[badarayana|Bādarāyaṇa]].[^1] They are one of the primary texts of the [[vedanta|Vedānta]] school, whose name refers to the end (*anta*) of the Vedas.[^2] Advaita Vedānta counts them, with the Upaniṣads and the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]], as its triple canon (*prasthānatrayī*).[^1] The major commentators of Vedānta, from Śaṅkara in the 8th century to Vallabha in the 16th, all wrote commentaries on the sūtras.[^2]

## Structure and contents

The work is divided into four *adhyāyas*. In the text on which [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]] commented, these hold 134, 157, 186 and 78 sūtras, 555 in all; other recensions differ slightly, and a second GRETIL edition has 545.[^3] Commentators group the sūtras into topics (*adhikaraṇa*), each of one or more sūtras.[^4]

## Key teachings

The sūtras are terse, and they are read through their commentaries. The work opens by announcing an enquiry into [[brahman]], and its second sūtra gives a first characterisation of brahman, as that from which the world arises:

> अथातो ब्रह्मजिज्ञासा\
> जन्माद्यस्य यतः
>
> *athāto brahmajijñāsā*\
> *janmādyasya yataḥ*
>
> "Then therefore the enquiry into Brahman. (Brahman is that) from which the origin, &c. (i.e. the origin, subsistence, and dissolution) of this (world proceed)." (tr. G. Thibaut, 1890)
>
> — Brahma Sūtra 1.1.1–2[^5]

The commentators differ on how brahman relates to individual selves and to the world; the entry on [[brahman]] sets out the main positions.

## Authorship and dating

The sūtras are ascribed to Bādarāyaṇa. Dalal places him around the first century BCE,[^1] while the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates the composition of the work to the first century CE.[^2]

## Commentaries and interpretations

Three commentaries are especially famous: Śaṅkara's from the 8th century (Advaita), [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]]'s from the 12th century (Viśiṣṭādvaita), and [[madhvacharya|Madhva]]'s from the 13th century (Dvaita). Later commentators include Nimbārka in the 13th century and Vallabha in the 16th.[^2] Śaṅkara's [[brahma-sutra-bhashya-shankara|commentary]] is the earliest complete commentary that survives.[^1] Rāmānuja's commentary is known as the *Śrī Bhāṣya*.[^6]

## Influence

Writing in 1890, the translator George Thibaut observed that the commentaries on the sūtras had become too numerous to list, because "at present, at any rate, there are very few Hindu sects not interested in showing that their distinctive tenets are countenanced by Badarayana's teaching".[^4]

## Editions and translations

George Thibaut translated the sūtras with Śaṅkara's commentary into English for the *Sacred Books of the East* (vol. 34, 1890; the work continues in vol. 38).[^4] GRETIL offers romanised e-texts of the sūtras and of several commentaries, and Sanskrit Wikisource gives the sūtras in Devanagari.[^3][^5]
