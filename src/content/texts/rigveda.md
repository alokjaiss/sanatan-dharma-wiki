## Overview

The **Rigveda** (*Ṛgveda*; ऋग्वेद) is the oldest of the four Vedas.[^1] It is a collection of hymns (*sūkta*) composed in Sanskrit, most of them praising deities such as Indra, Agni and Varuṇa.[^1] In the Pūrvamīmāṃsā and Vedānta traditions the Vedas are *śruti*, "heard" or revealed texts, and are contrasted with *smṛti*, "remembered" texts that rest on Vedic learning.[^2] The Rigveda has been transmitted orally through the ages with little change in its language and intonation.[^1]

## Structure and contents

Of the several recensions (*śākhā*) of the Rigveda, only that of the Śākala school has come down to us, with 1,028 hymns divided into ten books called *maṇḍalas*, "circles".[^1] Most hymns are in books 2 to 7, the "family books", each traditionally ascribed to one family of seers: book 2 to the family of Gṛtsamada, book 3 to Viśvāmitra, 4 to Vāmadeva, 5 to Atri, 6 to Bharadvāja and 7 to Vasiṣṭha, while book 8 is ascribed to the Kaṇva family.[^1] Book 9 gathers the hymns to Soma, and books 1 and 10 are ascribed to many different seers.[^1]

A second division, into eight *aṣṭakas* of eight *adhyāyas* each, is purely mechanical: it serves memorisation and has no connection with the meaning of the text.[^1] Hymns outside the main collection are called *khila*, "supplementary".[^1]

The hymns are of many kinds. Besides hymns of praise and hymns used in the sacrifice, there are verses for marriage and funeral rites; hymns on the creation of the world such as the Puruṣasūkta (10.90) and the Nāsadīyasūkta (10.129); dialogue hymns such as those of Purūravas and Urvaśī (10.95) and of Yama and Yamī (10.10); and hymns on everyday subjects, such as the Frog hymn (7.103) and the Dice hymn (10.34).[^1]

## Key teachings

The Rigveda is a collection rather than a single treatise, and its hymns come from different periods and serve different purposes.[^1] Its speculative hymns on the origin of the world, such as the Puruṣasūkta and the Nāsadīyasūkta, stand beside hymns of praise and prayer.[^1] One verse of hymn 1.164 speaks of the One to which the sages give many names:

> इन्द्रं मित्रं वरुणमग्निमाहुरथो दिव्यः स सुपर्णो गरुत्मान् ।\
> एकं सद्विप्रा बहुधा वदन्त्यग्निं यमं मातरिश्वानमाहुः ॥
>
> *indraṃ mitraṃ varuṇamagnimāhuratho divyaḥ sa suparṇo garutmān*\
> *ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadantyagniṃ yamaṃ mātariśvānamāhuḥ*
>
> "They call him Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and he is heavenly nobly-winged Garutman. To what is One, sages give many a title: they call it Agni, Yama, Matarisvan." (tr. R. T. H. Griffith, 1896)
>
> — Ṛgveda 1.164.46[^3]

## Authorship and dating

### Traditional account

The Sanskrit word *ṛṣi*, sage, is understood as "seer", because according to tradition the hymns were not composed but seen by the sages; the Rigveda is accordingly a revealed text.[^1] For the Pūrvamīmāṃsā school the Vedas are an eternally existing, unauthored corpus.[^2]

### Historical perspective

The language of the Rigveda shows that it is not a single, unitary work but combines older and later elements; the family books are generally held to be older than the others.[^1] The nomination of its manuscripts to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register describes the text as belonging to antiquity, "certainly not later than the second millennium B.C.E.", and as preserving expression "more than three thousand years old".[^1]

## Commentaries and interpretations

The *padapāṭha* gives the text word by word, separating each word from the euphonic combination (*sandhi*) of continuous recitation, which helps readers identify every word.[^1] Sāyaṇa's commentary is one of the earliest commentaries on the Rigveda.[^1]

Western scholars have read the hymns in different ways. Kaegi saw most of them as the simple outpouring of poets' hearts; Hermann Oldenberg took them for sacrificial songs and litanies composed by priests; Moriz Winternitz brought the two views together, holding that the hymns were of different types and from different periods.[^1]

## Influence

The four Vedas are traditionally regarded as pillars of Indian culture.[^1] The tradition of Vedic chanting, in which practitioners are taught from childhood complex recitation techniques based on tonal accents, precise pronunciation and specific combinations of words, was proclaimed by UNESCO in 2003 and inscribed on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.[^4]

Thirty Rigveda manuscripts at the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune were nominated to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register. They are written in Śāradā and Devanagari scripts on birch bark and paper, and the oldest is dated 1464 CE.[^1]

## Editions and translations

F. Max Müller's edition of the Rigveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary first appeared in 1849; he used one of the Bhandarkar Institute's manuscripts (no. 5/1875-76) to prepare it.[^1] The romanised GRETIL e-text is based on Theodor Aufrecht's edition of 1877.[^5] R. T. H. Griffith's English translation, *The Hymns of the Rigveda*, is in the public domain.[^3]
