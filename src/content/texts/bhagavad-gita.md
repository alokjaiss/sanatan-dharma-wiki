## Overview

The **Bhagavad Gita** (*Bhagavadgītā*; भगवद्गीता) occurs at the start of the sixth book of the [[mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], one of South Asia's two main epics.[^1] Its lead characters are the warrior Arjuna and his royal cousin [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]], who has offered to be his charioteer. The text opens on the battlefield of the war between kin that is the climax of the epic, and the dialogue amounts to a teaching that Kṛṣṇa gives Arjuna on their chariot.[^1] Although it forms part of the Mahābhārata, its importance in the tradition has made it widely regarded as a text in its own right.[^2]

## Structure and contents

The Gītā has eighteen chapters, which were originally untitled.[^1] In the critical edition of the Mahābhārata prepared at the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute (BORI), it has 700 verses and occupies chapters 23 to 40 of the sixth book.[^3]

## Key teachings

The Gītā responds to Arjuna's crisis on the battlefield with a teaching about action. It holds that it is better to perform one's own duty (*svadharma*) imperfectly than another's duty well (3.35), and it later connects a person's duties with their nature (18.41).[^1] It also teaches action without desire for its results, and discusses devotion to the Lord (*bhakti*) and knowledge (*jñāna*) alongside the discipline of action.[^1] The best-known statement of the teaching on action comes in the second chapter:

> कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन ।\
> मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि ॥
>
> *karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadā cana*\
> *mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgo ’stv akarmaṇi*
>
> "Your business is with action alone; not by any means with fruit. Let not the fruit of action be your motive (to action). Let not your attachment be (fixed) on inaction." (tr. K. T. Telang, 1882)
>
> — Bhagavad Gītā 2.47[^4]

## Authorship and dating

### Traditional account

The Mahābhārata presents itself as the work of Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana, [[vyasa|Vyāsa]], and the Gītā is traditionally read as part of that work.[^5] The epic places the battle between the Kauravas and the Pāṇḍavas, at whose outbreak the Gītā is spoken, in the interval between the Dvāpara and Kali yugas, at Samantapañcaka.[^5]

### Historical perspective

Both epics appear to have gone through a long process of revision and growth before their final Sanskrit versions appeared in the first two centuries of the common era.[^2] The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes the Mahābhārata as formulated at the start of the common era.[^1]

## Commentaries and interpretations

The major Vedānta philosophers, [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]], [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]] and [[madhvacharya|Madhva]], all wrote commentaries on the Gītā.[^1] Śaṅkara's is the earliest complete commentary on it that survives.[^6] With the Upaniṣads and the [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]], the Gītā forms the triple canon (*prasthānatrayī*) of Advaita Vedānta.[^6] Sanskrit Wikisource's edition prints each verse with several traditional commentaries, including those of Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī and Puruṣottama.[^7]

## Influence

Under British colonial rule the Gītā came increasingly to be seen as a separate, stand-alone religious book, a shift from how it had been understood within the philosophical tradition.[^1] Among modern readings, the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy discusses Gandhi's interpretation of its ideal of acting without desire for results.[^1]

## Editions and translations

The romanised GRETIL e-text follows the BORI critical edition of the Mahābhārata, from text entered by Muneo Tokunaga and others and revised by John Smith.[^3] K. T. Telang's English translation of 1882, published as volume 8 of the *Sacred Books of the East*, is in the public domain.[^4]
