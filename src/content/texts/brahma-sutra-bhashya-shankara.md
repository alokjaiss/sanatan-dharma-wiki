## Overview

The **Brahma Sutra Bhashya** (*Brahmasūtrabhāṣya*; ब्रह्मसूत्रभाष्य) is [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]]'s commentary on the [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]] of Bādarāyaṇa. It is the earliest complete commentary on the sūtras that survives.[^1] Together with Śaṅkara's commentaries on the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]] and the ten principal Upaniṣads, and his independent *Upadeśasāhasrī*, it belongs to the small group of works that define Śaṅkara's identity for historians, as distinct from the hundreds of later texts attributed to him.[^1] GRETIL gives its title as *Śārīrakamīmāṃsābhāṣya*.[^3]

## Structure and contents

The commentary follows the sūtras in order. It opens with an introduction known as the "Commentary on Superimposition" (*Adhyāsabhāṣya*).[^1] There Śaṅkara describes superimposition (*adhyāsa*) as the apprehension of something as something else.[^2]

## Key teachings

The commentary sets out the central claims of [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]]:

- The Upaniṣads reveal a principle of nonduality, [[brahman]], which is the reality of all things, and the essential core of one's self ([[atman|ātman]]) is brahman.[^1]
- The self is pure, non-intentional consciousness. Through ignorance (*avidyā*), the self and the not-self are mutually superimposed, and Śaṅkara identifies this ignorance as the root cause of all existential suffering.[^1]
- Reality is approached in two tiers: the conventional, empirical reality of the world (*vyāvahārika*) and the ultimate reality of nondual existence, which is brahman (*pāramārthika*).[^1]
- Knowledge of brahman alone liberates, and the seeker prepares for it through four qualifications: discrimination between the permanent and the impermanent, dispassion towards the fruits of action, disciplines such as calm and self-control, and a longing for liberation.[^2]

## Authorship and dating

Scholars treat the commentary as one of Śaṅkara's authentic works.[^1] It therefore shares his date, which most scholars place in the 8th century CE; proposals range from the late 7th century to 788–820 CE (see [[adi-shankaracharya|Ādi Śaṅkarācārya]]).[^1][^2]

## Commentaries and interpretations

The commentary generated a commentarial literature of its own. [[padmapada|Padmapāda]]'s *Pañcapādikā* comments on its treatment of the first sūtras.[^2] Two major sub-schools of Advaita grew from the sub-commentaries:[^2]

- the **Bhāmatī** school, named after Vācaspati Miśra's 9th-century commentary on Śaṅkara's work, holds that the individual self (*jīva*) is the locus and object of ignorance;
- the **Vivaraṇa** school, named after Prakāśātman's 10th-century commentary on the *Pañcapādikā*, holds that brahman is the locus of ignorance.

## Influence

Through this and his other commentaries, Śaṅkara became the most authoritative philosopher of Advaita Vedānta. An unbroken lineage beginning with his direct disciples continued to develop and defend his philosophy into the modern period.[^1]

## Editions and translations

GRETIL offers a romanised e-text of the commentary, adhyāya by adhyāya.[^3] George Thibaut's English translation appeared in the *Sacred Books of the East* (vol. 34, 1890, continued in vol. 38) and is in the public domain.[^4]
