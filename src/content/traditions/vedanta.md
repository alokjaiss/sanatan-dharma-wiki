## Overview

**Vedanta** (*Vedānta*; वेदान्त) is the school of Hindu philosophy concerned with the end (*anta*) of the Vedas, and one of the six āstika darśanas, the schools that recognise the authority of the Vedas.[^1] Its primary texts are the Upaniṣads, the [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]] of [[badarayana|Bādarāyaṇa]], and the commentarial traditions that interpret them.[^1] With the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]], the Upaniṣads and the Brahma Sūtras form the triple canon (*prasthānatrayī*) of Advaita Vedānta.[^2]

## History

The Brahma Sūtras are among Vedānta's primary texts. Dalal dates their author to about the first century BCE, while the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates the work to the first century CE.[^2][^1] Vedānta then developed as a series of commentaries on these texts. The three most famous are those of [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]] (8th century, Advaita), [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]] (12th century, Viśiṣṭādvaita) and [[madhvacharya|Madhva]] (13th century, Dvaita). Later commentators include Nimbārka in the 13th century and Vallabha in the 16th.[^1]

Before Śaṅkara, [[gaudapada|Gauḍapāda]] (6th century) composed verses on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad that are considered the first complete Advaita work.[^2][^5] Writing in 1890, George Thibaut observed that "very few Hindu sects" were not interested in showing that their tenets agreed with Bādarāyaṇa's teaching, and that the commentaries had become too numerous to list.[^6]

## Core doctrines

The Upaniṣads identify [[brahman]] with the self ([[atman|ātman]]), and suggest that knowing it saves one from all sorrow.[^1] The schools of Vedānta agree on the authority of these texts but differ on how brahman relates to individual selves and to the world:

- **[[advaita-vedanta|Advaita]]** (nonduality): brahman alone is ultimately real; the world of plurality appears through *māyā*; and the individual self is identical with brahman.[^5]
- **[[vishishtadvaita|Viśiṣṭādvaita]]** ("qualified non-dualism"): brahman stands to all other beings as a soul stands to its body.[^3]
- **[[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]]** (dualism): brahman alone is independent reality (*svatantra*), while selves and matter are real but dependent (*paratantra*), and there is a fivefold difference between God, selves and matter.[^4]

## Scriptural basis

All the major schools base their teaching on the same texts. Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva each wrote commentaries on the Brahma Sūtras,[^1] and each also commented on the Bhagavad Gītā.[^7] Śaṅkara's commentaries on the Brahma Sūtras, the Gītā and the ten principal Upaniṣads are the earliest complete ones that survive.[^2]

## Institutions and centres

Śaṅkara is said to have established the Daśanāmī renouncer orders and four or five principal monasteries across the subcontinent.[^2] Among them is the [[sringeri-sharada-peetham|Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham]] in Karnataka. Madhva's Dvaita tradition is kept alive by a community whose main centre is at Uḍupi, also in Karnataka.[^4]

## Relations with other traditions

Vedānta shares with Pūrvamīmāṃsā the view that the Vedas are *śruti*, revealed texts.[^1] Maṇḍana Miśra, an important early Advaitin of the 8th century, had earlier been an adherent of the rival Bhāṭṭa school of Mīmāṃsā.[^5]
