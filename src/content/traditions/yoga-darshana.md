## Overview

The **Yoga Darshana** (*Yoga darśana*; योग दर्शन) is one of the six āstika darśanas of Hindu philosophy.[^1] Its foundational text is [[patanjali|Patañjali]]'s [[yoga-sutra|Yoga Sūtras]], read through the commentary ascribed to Vyāsa.[^2] Yoga shares much with [[samkhya|Sāṅkhya]], above all its dualist picture of conscious selves and primordial matter. It departs from Sāṅkhya, however, on the nature of agency and in its emphasis on practical means.[^1]

## History

The origins of yoga long preceded Patañjali, who is not its founder.[^2] Most scholars date his Yoga Sūtras to around the 1st–2nd century CE, and they are no later than the 5th century CE.[^2] The school's understanding of the sūtras was shaped by its commentaries:[^2]

- the [[yoga-bhashya|Yoga Bhāṣya]] ascribed to Vyāsa (around the 4th–5th century), which became almost as authoritative as the sūtras;
- Vācaspati Miśra's *Tattvavaiśāradī* (9th century);
- King Bhoja's *Rājamārtaṇḍa* (11th century);
- Vijñānabhikṣu's *Yogavārttika*.

## Core doctrines

- **Two realities.** The universe is the product of two distinct categories: *prakṛti*, the primordial material matrix, and *puruṣa*, the innumerable conscious selves embedded within it.[^2]
- **Yoga as stilling the mind.** Patañjali defines yoga as the stilling of all the states of the mind (*citta-vṛtti-nirodha*, 1.2).[^2]
- **Īśvara.** Yoga recognises the Lord, Īśvara, as a special self. Īśvara is untouched by karma and the afflictions, omniscient, and the teacher of the ancients, and devotion to Īśvara is one way to the goal (1.23).[^2][^3]
- **Liberation (*kaivalya*).** The goal is to free the self from its entanglement with matter, and the last chapter of the Yoga Sūtras is devoted to this liberation.[^2]

## Scriptural basis

The Yoga Sūtras, 195 sūtras in four chapters, are the school's root text. In practice, the philosophy of Patañjali means Patañjali as Vyāsa understood him, since every later commentator built on Vyāsa's commentary.[^2][^3]

## Practice

Yoga sets out an eightfold path (2.29): abstentions (*yama*), observances (*niyama*), posture (*āsana*), breath control (*prāṇāyāma*), withdrawal of the senses (*pratyāhāra*), concentration (*dhāraṇā*), meditation (*dhyāna*) and absorption (*samādhi*).[^2][^3] In the premodern tradition these limbs served meditation. The presentation of yoga as mainly bodily posture is essentially a modern Western development.[^2]

## Relations with other traditions

Sāṅkhya and Yoga share the same metaphysics and the same goal of freeing the self, but differ in method. Sāṅkhya follows the path of reasoning, analysing the constituents of matter from which the self is to be freed. Yoga follows the path of meditation, attending to the nature of mind and consciousness.[^2] The last chapter of the Yoga Sūtras also responds to Buddhist challenges.[^2] A commentary on the Yoga Sūtras is attributed to the Advaita teacher Śaṅkara, though its authenticity has been questioned since 1927.[^2]
