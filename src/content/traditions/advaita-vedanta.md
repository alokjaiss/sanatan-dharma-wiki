## Overview

**Advaita Vedanta** (*Advaita Vedānta*; अद्वैत वेदान्त) is the school of [[vedanta|Vedānta]] that teaches nonduality. *Advaita* is often translated "non-dualism", though it literally means "non-secondness".[^1] It is a radical nondualism, a revisionary worldview that it derives from the ancient Upaniṣads. On its reading, the Upaniṣads reveal a single principle, [[brahman]], which is the reality of all things, and they establish that the essential core of the self ([[atman|ātman]]) is brahman.[^2]

## History

An Advaita tradition may already have existed in the early part of the first millennium CE: [[adi-shankaracharya|Śaṅkara]] himself refers to a tradition (*sampradāya*).[^1] The only earlier names with some historical certainty are [[gaudapada|Gauḍapāda]], the teacher of Śaṅkara's teacher, and [[govinda-bhagavatpada|Govinda Bhagavatpāda]], Śaṅkara's own teacher.[^1] Gauḍapāda's commentary in verse on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad is considered the first complete Advaita work.[^1]

Śaṅkara, whom many scholars place in the 8th century, gave the school its most sustained and authoritative statement in his commentaries on the Upaniṣads, the [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]] and the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]].[^1][^2] His pupils [[padmapada|Padmapāda]] and [[sureshvara|Sureśvara]] extended his work.[^1] Maṇḍana Miśra (8th century), who had earlier been an adherent of the Bhāṭṭa school of Mīmāṃsā, set out a distinct form of Advaita in his *Brahmasiddhi*.[^1]

Two major sub-schools arose after Śaṅkara.[^1] The **Bhāmatī** school is named after Vācaspati Miśra's 9th-century commentary on Śaṅkara's commentary on the Brahma Sūtras. The **Vivaraṇa** school is named after Prakāśātman's 10th-century commentary on Padmapāda's *Pañcapādikā*. Later Advaitins include Vimuktātman and Sarvajñātman in the 10th century, Vidyāraṇya in the 14th and Madhusūdana Sarasvatī in the 17th.[^1]

## Core doctrines

- **Brahman alone is real.** Brahman is the fundamental reality underlying all objects and experiences. It is explained as pure existence, pure consciousness and pure bliss.[^1]
- **The self is brahman.** The experiencing self (*jīva*) and the self of the universe (*ātman*) are in reality identical: both are brahman.[^1] A well-known anonymous verse sums up these doctrines: brahman alone is true, the world of plurality is an error, and the individual self is not different from brahman.[^1]
- **[[maya|Māyā]].** Brahman appears as the manifold objects of experience through its creative power, *māyā*: what appears real at the time of experience but has no ultimate existence.[^1] The world is an apparent transformation (*vivarta*), a superimposition on brahman.[^1]
- **Ignorance and superimposition.** Ignorance (*avidyā*) confounds self and not-self.[^1] Śaṅkara identifies it as the root cause of all existential suffering.[^2] The school's sub-traditions disagree about where ignorance resides. For the Bhāmatī school its locus is the individual self; for the Vivaraṇa school it is brahman.[^1]
- **Levels of reality.** Classical Advaita distinguishes three planes of existence: absolute (*pāramārthika*), worldly (*vyāvahārika*) and illusory (*prātibhāsika*).[^1]

## Scriptural basis

The Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Brahma Sūtras form Advaita Vedānta's triple canon (*prasthānatrayī*).[^2] Śaṅkara's commentaries on all three are the earliest complete commentaries on them that survive.[^2]

## Practice

For Śaṅkara, knowledge of brahman alone leads to liberation.[^1] He sets out four qualifications that prepare the seeker for this knowledge:[^1]

- discrimination (*viveka*) between the permanent and the impermanent;
- dispassion towards the fruits of action, in this world and in heaven;
- disciplines such as calm and self-control;
- a longing for liberation.

## Lineage (paramparā)

The tradition traces its teachers from Gauḍapāda through Govinda to Śaṅkara.[^2] Śaṅkara is said to have had four prominent pupils: Padmapāda, Sureśvara, Hastāmalaka and Toṭaka.[^1] An unbroken lineage beginning with his direct disciples continued to develop and defend his philosophy into the modern period.[^2]

## Institutions and centres

Śaṅkara is said to have founded the Daśanāmī ("ten names") renouncer orders and four or five principal monasteries across the subcontinent.[^2] The [[sringeri-sharada-peetham|Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham]] regards itself as the first of four *āmnāya* pīṭhas he established, with Sureśvara as its first ācārya.[^3] The Kāñci Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham traces an unbroken line of 71 ācāryas from Śaṅkara.[^4]

## Relations with other traditions

Later schools of Vedānta read the same texts differently. For [[vishishtadvaita|Viśiṣṭādvaita]], brahman is related to all beings as a soul to its body.[^5] For [[dvaita-vedanta|Dvaita]], brahman alone is independent reality, and selves and matter are real but dependent.[^6]
