## Overview

**Adi Shankaracharya** (*Ādi Śaṅkarācārya*; आदि शङ्कराचार्य), usually called Śaṅkara, is the preeminent teacher of [[advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]]. He is among the most widely known and influential Indian philosophers of the classical period, and the most authoritative philosopher of his school.[^1] Advaita holds that the Upaniṣads reveal a single, nondual reality, [[brahman]], and that the essential core of the self ([[atman|ātman]]) is brahman. This philosophy finds its earliest sustained articulation in Śaṅkara's works.[^1]

## Life

### Traditional account

The traditional accounts of Śaṅkara's life, the *Śaṅkaravijaya*s ("Conquests of Śaṅkara"), were composed several centuries after his death.[^1] They describe him as born at [[kaladi|Kaladi]], in present-day Kerala. He became a renouncer at a young age, left his family to seek liberation, and spent his life travelling across the subcontinent, teaching his pupils, debating, and composing his works. He is said to have died at the age of thirty-two.[^1]

The tradition holds that Śaṅkara founded the monastic Daśanāmī ("ten names") renouncer orders and four or five principal monasteries (*maṭha*s) across the subcontinent.[^1] According to the [[sringeri-sharada-peetham|Śṛṅgeri Śāradā Pīṭham]], he established it, more than twelve centuries ago, as the first of four *āmnāya* pīṭhas in the four corners of the country, and appointed his disciple [[sureshvara|Sureśvara]] as its first ācārya.[^3] The Kāñci Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham gives a different chronology. On its account, Śaṅkara was born at Kaladi in 509 BCE, founded the Kāñci pīṭha in 482 BCE, and attained liberation in his thirty-second year, in 477 BCE.[^4]

### Historical perspective

Scholarly dates for Śaṅkara vary. Early scholars placed him in 788–820 CE. Nakamura argued for 700–750 CE, and Potter for the late 7th to early 8th century CE.[^1] Many scholars place him in the eighth century.[^2] Historians define Śaṅkara by the works that can be securely attributed to him (see *Works*), and treat the hagiographies as later traditions.[^1]

## Teachings

For Śaṅkara, the self is pure, non-intentional consciousness, and it is identical with brahman.[^1] Human beings superimpose the self and the not-self on each other. This failure is caused by ignorance (*avidyā*), which Śaṅkara identifies as the root cause of all existential suffering.[^1] He approaches reality in two tiers: the conventional, empirical reality of the world (*vyāvahārika*) and the ultimate reality of nondual existence, which is brahman (*pāramārthika*).[^1]

Liberation comes through knowledge of brahman alone.[^2] The seeker prepares for it with four qualifications: discrimination between the permanent and the impermanent, dispassion towards the fruits of action here and hereafter, disciplines such as calm and self-control, and a longing for liberation.[^2]

## Works

From a historical standpoint, Śaṅkara's identity is defined by a small group of works. These are the earliest complete surviving commentaries on the [[brahma-sutra|Brahma Sūtras]], the [[bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]] and the ten principal Upaniṣads, together with an independent work, the *Upadeśasāhasrī* ("A Thousand Teachings").[^1] Hundreds of other texts are attributed to him. Their authorship is doubtful, and most were probably composed in later centuries by heads of monasteries who held the title "Śaṅkarācārya".[^1]

## Lineage

Śaṅkara's teacher was [[govinda-bhagavatpada|Govinda]]. According to tradition, Govinda's teacher was [[gaudapada|Gauḍapāda]] (6th century CE), who composed verses on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad.[^1] Śaṅkara is said to have had four prominent pupils: [[padmapada|Padmapāda]], Sureśvara, Hastāmalaka and Toṭaka.[^2]

## Legacy and institutions

An unbroken lineage, beginning with direct disciples such as Padmapāda and Sureśvara, continued to develop, defend and extend Śaṅkara's philosophy into the modern period.[^1] Two later sub-schools of Advaita, the Bhāmatī and the Vivaraṇa, grew out of commentaries on his commentary on the Brahma Sūtras.[^2] Heads of the monasteries associated with him have held the title "Śaṅkarācārya";[^1] at Śṛṅgeri the pontiffs are styled Jagadguru Śaṅkarācārya.[^3]
